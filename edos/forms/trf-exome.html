General information about genetic testing

What is genetic testing?

Genetic conditions come from changes in a person's DNA. DNA carries the instructions for how the body grows and works. It is packed into 46 chromosomes, found in almost every cell. A gene is a stretch of DNA with the instructions for making a protein. Genetic testing looks for changes in chromosomes or genes. Its purpose here is to see whether the patient has a genetic change that explains their condition, or to estimate the chance of passing one on. In this form "patient" can also mean an unborn child. Your doctor can give more information about the specific test ordered.

What could I learn?

Please tell the laboratory if a gene change or chromosome rearrangement is already known in the family. A test can give one of three kinds of result:
  1. Positive. A gene or chromosome change was found that explains the condition, or that raises the chance of a condition in future.
  2. Negative. No disease-causing change was found by this test. This does not guarantee good health, and does not rule out a genetic cause the test could not detect.
  3. Uncertain (variant of uncertain significance). A change was found but it is not yet known whether it causes disease. It is not the same as a positive result. More tests on parents or relatives, and detailed medical records, may help. Medical knowledge changes, so the laboratory does not routinely re-analyse old results; you or your doctor can ask for a review.

Risks and limits

Genetic tests do not always give a clear answer. A change may exist and not be found, because of the limits of current knowledge and technology. If family relationships are not as stated, the result can be misread. Results are read together with clinical findings, family history and other tests, and only changes in genes linked to the patient's condition are reported. Rarely, results can be wrong, for example from a mislabelled sample, wrong clinical information, a rare technical error, or a recent bone marrow transplant or transfusion. The test cannot predict every long-term health risk. Some regions of the exome are not covered well enough to be read; coverage details are available on request. Large deletions or duplications, abnormal methylation, repeat expansions and similar changes are not routinely reported. A new sample may be needed if the first is not adequate. Turnaround time depends on the complexity of the test.

Prenatal samples

For prenatal testing, the fetal sample is checked for contamination with maternal cells. A maternal blood sample is strongly encouraged for this check.

Counselling

Genetic counselling before and after testing is recommended. The report does not diagnose a condition or recommend treatment; please review it with your doctor.
